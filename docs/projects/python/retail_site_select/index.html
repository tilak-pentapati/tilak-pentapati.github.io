1 Executive Summary & Retail Location Problem

The rapid growth of e-commerce and hybrid Click & Collect (C&C) grocery fulfilment has transformed urban retail location strategy. Rather than relying solely on large out-of-town superstores, major UK grocery retailers increasingly deploy neighbourhood convenience stores (c-stores) equipped with Click & Collect facilities to capture time-poor commuters, small urban households, and digitally engaged neighbourhoods.

In this assignment, we conduct a multi-stage GIS site-selection analysis across Liverpool (E08000012, 298 LSOAs) to identify the optimal location for a new Marks & Spencer (M&S) convenience store with a Click & Collect (C&C) facility. Originally developed across QGIS, ArcGIS Pro (Network Analyst), and R, the entire spatial workflow and cartographic output series (map1 through map6) are reproduced here directly from the underlying shapefiles in Python.

4.1 Map 1: Online Grocery Shopping Prevalence Across Liverpool LSOAs (IUC 2018)

The Consumer Data Research Centre (CDRC) 2018 Internet User Classification (IUC 2018) segments Great Britain neighbourhoods into 10 distinct digital engagement profiles using Small Area Estimation (SAE) and gradient-boosted decision trees trained on British Population Survey (BPS) online shopping transactions, Ofcom broadband infrastructure, and ONS Census demographics.

Based on each IUC group’s Buy Groceries Online propensity z-score in the CDRC User Guide, Liverpool’s 298 LSOAs are classified into four ordinal Online Grocery Shopping Prevalence tiers (OrdinalTes):

  • High Prevalence (4, Blue Hatching ///): e-Cultural Creators (GRP 1, z = +1.6), e-Professionals (GRP 2, z = +1.4), e-Veterans (GRP 3, z = +0.9), and e-Rational Utilitarians (GRP 5, z = +0.65) — 61 LSOAs (20.5\%).
  • Moderate Prevalence (3, Green Hatching ///): e-Mainstream (GRP 6, z = +0.05) — 38 LSOAs (12.8\%).
  • Low Prevalence (2, Orange Hatching ///): Digital Seniors (GRP 8, z = -0.10) and Settled Offline Communities (GRP 9, z = -0.35) — 8 LSOAs (2.7\%).
  • Very Low Prevalence (1, Red Hatching ///): Youthful Urban Fringe (GRP 4, z = -0.45), Passive and Uncommitted Users (GRP 7, z = -0.45), and e-Withdrawn (GRP 10, z = -1.60) — 191 LSOAs (64.1\%).

4.2 Map 2: Overlaying Deprivation (IMD 2019) with Online Grocery Shopping Prevalence

A new Marks & Spencer C-Store with Click & Collect requires a catchment that is not only digitally engaged (High Prevalence) but also economically affluent enough to sustain premium convenience expenditure. In Map 2, we classify the English Index of Multiple Deprivation (IMD 2019) Rank across Liverpool (1 = most deprived in England, 32,844 = least deprived) into three tiers:

  1. More Deprived (Rank 10 – 1,416, Solid Red): Severely deprived neighbourhoods concentrated in north Liverpool (Kirkdale, Everton, Anfield, Walton) and outer social housing estates (Speke, Norris Green).
  2. Moderate (Rank 1,416 – 15,000, Salmon Pink): Transitional middle-income urban neighbourhoods.
  3. More Affluent (Rank 15,000 – 31,562, White): Affluent suburban and waterfront neighbourhoods (Allerton Road, Mossley Hill, Childwall, Woolton, Wavertree, and Liverpool City Centre Waterfront).

Overlaying the hatched Online Grocery Shopping Prevalence layer reveals a striking spatial inverse correlation: High Online Grocery Shopping Prevalence (blue hatching over white/pink background) clusters tightly in south-central and central Liverpool around the Allerton Road, Smithdown Road, Woolton, and City Centre corridors, as well as an affluent pocket north of Old Swan.

## Executive Summary & Retail Location Problem

The rapid growth of e-commerce and hybrid **Click & Collect (C&C)** grocery fulfilment has transformed urban retail location strategy. Rather than relying solely on large out-of-town superstores, major UK grocery retailers increasingly deploy neighbourhood **convenience stores (`c-stores`) equipped with Click & Collect facilities** to capture time-poor commuters, small urban households, and digitally engaged neighbourhoods.

## Stage 1: Demand Screening — Online Grocery Shopping & Deprivation

### Map 1: Online Grocery Shopping Prevalence Across Liverpool LSOAs (`IUC 2018`)

The Consumer Data Research Centre (CDRC) **2018 Internet User Classification (`IUC 2018`)** segments Great Britain neighbourhoods into 10 distinct digital engagement profiles using Small Area Estimation (SAE) and gradient-boosted decision trees trained on British Population Survey (BPS) online shopping transactions, Ofcom broadband infrastructure, and ONS Census demographics.

Based on each IUC group's *Buy Groceries Online* propensity $z$-score in the CDRC User Guide, Liverpool's 298 LSOAs are classified into four ordinal **Online Grocery Shopping Prevalence** tiers (`OrdinalTes`):

* **High Prevalence (`4`, Blue Hatching `///`):** *e-Cultural Creators* (`GRP 1`, $z = +1.6$), *e-Professionals* (`GRP 2`, $z = +1.4$), *e-Veterans* (`GRP 3`, $z = +0.9$), and *e-Rational Utilitarians* (`GRP 5`, $z = +0.65$) — $61$ LSOAs ($20.5\%$).
* **Moderate Prevalence (`3`, Green Hatching `///`):** *e-Mainstream* (`GRP 6`, $z = +0.05$) — $38$ LSOAs ($12.8\%$).
* **Low Prevalence (`2`, Orange Hatching `///`):** *Digital Seniors* (`GRP 8`, $z = -0.10$) and *Settled Offline Communities* (`GRP 9`, $z = -0.35$) — $8$ LSOAs ($2.7\%$).
* **Very Low Prevalence (`1`, Red Hatching `///`):** *Youthful Urban Fringe* (`GRP 4`, $z = -0.45$), *Passive and Uncommitted Users* (`GRP 7`, $z = -0.45$), and *e-Withdrawn* (`GRP 10`, $z = -1.60$) — $191$ LSOAs ($64.1\%$).

## Key Conclusions & Strategic Recommendations

1. **Optimal M&S Click & Collect Location:** The selected site at **`E: 340,650.3, N: 388,487.1`** (Wavertree / Childwall / North Allerton border) sits at the nexus of Liverpool's most affluent, digitally engaged residential suburbs (*e-Veterans* and *e-Professionals*, `IMD Rank > 18,000`) while maintaining high arterial road visibility and workplace accessibility.

3. **Superiority of Network Drive-Distance over Euclidean Buffers:** Whereas a $500\text{ m}$ Euclidean circle covers **$78.5\text{ ha}$** ($785,398\text{ m}^2$) by assuming isotropic straight-line travel across barriers, the **$500\text{ m}$ Road-Network Service Area** covers **$56.63\text{ ha}$** ($566,250\text{ m}^2$ — a **$27.9\%$ reduction**), accurately reflecting real street connectivity and physical barriers.
