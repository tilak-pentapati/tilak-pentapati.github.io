1.3 Dependent & Explanatory Variable Specification

Following the guidance in Chapter 7, we construct a consistent set of variables used across the cross-sectional models in Chapters 7, 8, and 9:

  1. Dependent Variable (covid19_r): We focus on the UK’s Third Wave (01 December 2020 to 29 January 2021, columns X2020.12.01 through X2021.01.29), driven by the Alpha (B.1.1.7) variant. We sum daily confirmed cases over this 60-day window (cum_covid) and compute the cumulative COVID-19 infection risk per 1,000 residents: \text{covid19\_r}_i = \text{round}\left(\frac{\text{cum\_covid}_i}{\text{Residents}_i} \times 1000\right)
  2. Explanatory Variables (Compositional Proportions): We select four theoretically grounded socio-demographic and housing proportions capturing local vulnerability and transmission exposure:
    • crowded_hou (\text{Crowded\_housing} / \text{Households}): Share of households living in overcrowded accommodation (\ge 1 person per room), which facilitates indoor household transmission.
    • elderly ((\text{Age\_65\_to\_84} + \text{Age\_85plus}) / \text{Residents}): Share of residents aged 65 and over, capturing demographic age structure and shielding behaviour.
    • ethnic ((\text{Mixed} + \text{Indian} + \text{Pakistani} + \text{Bangladeshi} + \text{Chinese} + \text{Other\_Asian} + \text{Black} + \text{Other\_ethnicity}) / \text{Residents}): Share of non-white ethnic minority population, reflecting structural, urban, and front-line occupational exposure disparities.
    • lt_illness (\text{Long\_term\_ill} / \text{Residents}): Share of residents whose day-to-day activities are limited by long-term illness or disability, capturing underlying health vulnerability and socioeconomic deprivation [1].

We also compute mean-centered versions of each predictor (z_x = x - \bar{x}) to aid interpretation of random-slope correlations in Chapter 8 [2].

2 Multilevel Modelling — Part 1: Varying Intercepts (Chapter 7 Tasks)

Standard OLS regression assumes observations are independent, ignoring the clustering of UTLADs within Regions and consequently underestimating standard errors [3]. We address this using two-level random-intercept models estimated via Restricted Maximum Likelihood (REML) in lme4.

2.1 Task 7.1: Varying-Intercept Null Model (m1_mlm)

We specify a two-level varying-intercept model with no explanatory variables, partitioning total variance in y_{ij} (covid19_r for UTLAD i in Region j) into a national fixed intercept (\beta_0), a Region-level random intercept deviation (u_{0j} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2_{u0})), and a UTLAD-level residual (e_{ij} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2_e)):

y_{ij} = \beta_0 + u_{0j} + e_{ij}

Code 
# Baseline intercept-only OLS for reference

2.3 Task 7.3: Variance Partition Coefficient (VPC)

The Variance Partition Coefficient (VPC) measures the proportion of total unexplained variance in covid19_r attributable to differences between Regions (Level 2):

\text{VPC} = \frac{\sigma^2_{u0}}{\sigma^2_{u0} + \sigma^2_e}

We extract the variance components from both m1_mlm and m2_mlm to compute their exact VPCs.

Code 
vc_m1 <- as.data.frame(VarCorr(m1_mlm))
vpc_m1 <- vc_m1$vcov[1] / sum(vc_m1$vcov)

vc_m2 <- as.data.frame(VarCorr(m2_mlm))
vpc_m2 <- vc_m2$vcov[1] / sum(vc_m2$vcov)

vpc_table <- tibble(
  Model = c("Model 1: Null Varying-Intercept", "Model 2: Varying-Intercept + 4 Predictors"),
  `Region Variance (Level 2)` = round(c(vc_m1$vcov[1], vc_m2$vcov[1]), 3),
  `Region SD` = round(c(vc_m1$sdcor[1], vc_m2$sdcor[1]), 3),
  `UTLAD Residual Variance (Level 1)` = round(c(vc_m1$vcov[2], vc_m2$vcov[2]), 3),
  `UTLAD Residual SD` = round(c(vc_m1$sdcor[2], vc_m2$sdcor[2]), 3),

Analysis and Discussion (Chapter 7):

  1. Extent of Variation Across Geographical Scales:
    • In the unconditional null model (m1_mlm), the between-region variance is 157.65 (\text{SD} = 12.56) and the within-region (between-UTLAD) variance is 115.8 (\text{SD} = 10.76), yielding a VPC of 57.7%. Thus, over 58% of the total variance in Wave-3 COVID-19 infection rates across England is explained by macro-scale differences between the 9 Regions—reflecting the regionalised emergence and spread of the Alpha variant in London, the South East, and the East of England during December 2020–January 2021.
    • Once the four UTLAD-level compositional predictors (crowded_hou, elderly, ethnic, lt_illness) are added in m2_mlm, both Level-2 regional variance (dropping to 128.8) and Level-1 UTLAD variance (dropping to 93.04) decrease substantially, reducing the conditional VPC to 58.1%. This shows that roughly 18.3% of the initial between-region variance was driven by regional differences in overcrowding, ethnic composition, age structure, and long-term illness. After accounting for composition, most remaining unexplained variance (41.9%) lies at the local UTLAD scale (Level 1).
  2. Interpretation of Fixed Effects and Varying Intercepts:
    • In m2_mlm, crowded_hou (\hat{\beta}_1 = 94.04), ethnic (\hat{\beta}_3 = 22), and lt_illness (\hat{\beta}_4 = 126.99) are positively associated with COVID-19 infection risk, whereas elderly (\hat{\beta}_2 = -55.8) is negatively associated after controlling for long-term illness (reflecting stricter shielding and lower workplace mobility among older cohorts).
    • The caterpillar plots (Figure 2.1) show statistically significant regional differences (bold confidence intervals not overlapping zero): in m1_mlm, London exhibits a large positive residual above the national average (\hat{u}_{0,\text{London}} = +23.91), while South West (\hat{u}_{0,\text{SW}} = -12.27) and Yorkshire and The Humber (\hat{u}_{0,\text{YH}} = -13.92) lie significantly below average. Crucially, in m2_mlm, once London’s high household overcrowding and ethnic diversity are controlled for, London’s regional intercept residual shrinks dramatically toward zero (\hat{u}_{0,\text{London}} = 17.15), whereas the North West, East of England, and South East remain significantly above average and the South West and Yorkshire and The Humber remain significantly below average.

Analysis and Discussion (Chapter 8):

  1. Comparison of Varying-Slope (m3_mlm) vs. Varying-Intercept + Varying-Slope (m4_mlm):
    • Which is better and why? The varying-intercept and varying-slope model (m4_mlm, or the parsimonious varying-intercept model m2_mlm_c) vastly outperforms the varying-slope-only model (m3_mlm). Specifically, m3_mlm yields an AIC of 1180.8 (deviance = 1166.8), whereas adding varying regional intercepts in m4_mlm lowers AIC dramatically to 1130.8 (deviance = 1112.8, p < 0.001). As emphasised by [3], forcing all regions to share a single baseline intercept (m3_mlm) while allowing only the slope to tilt severely misspecifies the large baseline differences in regional infection waves across England.
    • Comparing m2_mlm_c (varying-intercept) against m4_mlm (varying-intercept and varying-slope), adding the random slope and covariance parameters lowers deviance marginally, showing that while the relationship between long-term illness and COVID-19 exhibits regional heterogeneity (stronger positive slopes in northern industrial regions such as the North West and Yorkshire and The Humber), the dominant source of regional hierarchy is baseline intercept variation (u_{0j}).
  2. Varying Slope Estimates and Intercept-Slope Correlation:
    • Across all UTLADs, the fixed average slope for z_lt_illness in m4_mlm is strongly positive (\hat{\beta}_4 = 137.12), indicating that a 1 percentage point increase in long-term illness share is associated with roughly 1.37 additional COVID-19 cases per 1,000 residents on average.
    • The correlation between regional intercept residuals (u_{0j}) and regional slope residuals (u_{4j}) in m4_mlm captures how baseline regional wave intensity interacts with health deprivation sensitivity: northern regions (North West, North East) exhibit steeper positive slopes (where chronic health deprivation strongly amplifies local infection risk), whereas southern regions with lower prevalence (South West) exhibit flatter slopes.

  2. Appropriate Bandwidth Selection (Fixed vs. Adaptive Kernel):
    • The Adaptive Bandwidth (abw = 0.03) is theoretically and empirically superior to the Fixed Bandwidth (fbw = 46.4 km) for England’s UTLAD geography.
    • Why? English UTLADs vary drastically in physical area and centroid spacing: London comprises 32 compact boroughs within a ~25 km radius, and metropolitan regions (Greater Manchester, Merseyside, West Midlands, Tyne and Wear) also consist of tightly packed districts, whereas rural shire counties (Cumbria, Northumberland, Cornwall, Devon, North Yorkshire) span vast territories. A fixed distance kernel (46.4 km) encompasses dozens of dissimilar authorities in London (over-smoothing urban heterogeneity and Increasing bias) while capturing very few neighbours in rural Cornwall or Cumbria (inflating variance and standard errors). The adaptive kernel dynamically expands in sparse rural areas and contracts in dense metropolitan cores, achieving a lower AIC (940.2 vs. 1027.5) and higher Quasi-global R^2 (0.925 vs. 0.846).

5.4 Analysis and Discussion (Chapter 10)

  1. Comparison of GWR and Spatio-Temporal (ST) Model Results Across Space:
    • Consistency of Contextual Effects: Once space-time trends and FRK Gaussian basis functions (B1–B9) are controlled for in the Negative Binomial models (nb_m1 and nb_m2), the global contextual predictors align closely with the dominant local patterns identified by GWR in Chapter 9: crowded_hou, ethnic, and lt_illness exhibit positive and statistically significant associations with COVID-19 infection risk (p < 0.001), whereas elderly displays a statistically significant negative coefficient (p < 0.001).
    • How GWR and ST Capture Spatial Variation Differently: GWR (Chapter 9) models spatial non-stationarity by allowing the slopes of crowded_hou, elderly, ethnic, and lt_illness to vary locally across the 149 UTLADs within a single cross-sectional time window. By contrast, the FRK Spatio-Temporal model (Chapter 10) estimates global partial effects for the demographic covariates while decomposing continuous spatial variation and spatial dependence into a linear combination of radial basis functions (B1–B9, nearly all of which are highly statistically significant at p < 0.001 in nb_m1 and nb_m2) alongside explicit coordinate trends (long, lat) and space-time interactions.
    • Furthermore, the Poisson model exhibits severe overdispersion (\text{Deviance}/\text{df} = 19.2 \gg 1), which causes standard Poisson standard errors to be artificially small. Moving to the Negative Binomial (nb_m1 and nb_m2) reduces residual deviance from 1.71098^{5} to 9261, providing much more reliable statistical inference.
  2. Significant Variations Over Time and Space-Time Interactions:
    • Temporal Autocorrelation and Trend: The exploratory analysis demonstrates strong positive first-order temporal autocorrelation (r_{t, t-1} = 0.781) and a clear 7-day seasonal reporting cycle (Figure 5.4), alongside an inverted-U wave trajectory that surged through December 2020 and peaked in early January 2021 before declining under national lockdown.
    • Space-Time Interaction Terms (nb_m2): In nb_m1, the main effect of time_index is positive and highly statistically significant (\hat{\beta}_{\text{time}} = 0.0139, p < 0.001), confirming a significant upward temporal trend across the Wave-3 window. Even more revealingly, in nb_m2, the space-time interaction terms long:time_index and lat:time_index (as well as long:lat) are highly statistically significant:
      • The significant lat:time_index interaction captures the south-to-north diffusion of the Alpha variant during Wave 3: whereas early December 2020 cases were heavily concentrated in Kent, London, and the South East (lower latitude, higher longitude), infection rates accelerated northward and westward (long:time_index and lat:time_index) into the Midlands, North West, and Cumbria as time progressed through January 2021 (as visually confirmed by the weekly maps in Figure 5.1 and the Hovmöller diagram in Figure 5.3).

### Dependent & Explanatory Variable Specification

Following the guidance in Chapter 7, we construct a consistent set of variables used across the cross-sectional models in Chapters 7, 8, and 9:

1. **Dependent Variable (`covid19_r`)**: We focus on the UK's **Third Wave** (`01 December 2020` to `29 January 2021`, columns `X2020.12.01` through `X2021.01.29`), driven by the Alpha (B.1.1.7) variant. We sum daily confirmed cases over this 60-day window (`cum_covid`) and compute the cumulative COVID-19 infection risk per 1,000 residents:
   $$
   \text{covid19\_r}_i = \text{round}\left(\frac{\text{cum\_covid}_i}{\text{Residents}_i} \times 1000\right)
   $$
2. **Explanatory Variables (Compositional Proportions)**: We select four theoretically grounded socio-demographic and housing proportions capturing local vulnerability and transmission exposure:
   - `crowded_hou` ($\text{Crowded\_housing} / \text{Households}$): Share of households living in overcrowded accommodation ($\ge 1$ person per room), which facilitates indoor household transmission.
   - `elderly` ($(\text{Age\_65\_to\_84} + \text{Age\_85plus}) / \text{Residents}$): Share of residents aged 65 and over, capturing demographic age structure and shielding behaviour.
   - `ethnic` ($(\text{Mixed} + \text{Indian} + \text{Pakistani} + \text{Bangladeshi} + \text{Chinese} + \text{Other\_Asian} + \text{Black} + \text{Other\_ethnicity}) / \text{Residents}$): Share of non-white ethnic minority population, reflecting structural, urban, and front-line occupational exposure disparities.

## Multilevel Modelling — Part 1: Varying Intercepts (Chapter 7 Tasks)

Standard OLS regression assumes observations are independent, ignoring the clustering of UTLADs within Regions and consequently underestimating standard errors [@gelman2006data]. We address this using two-level random-intercept models estimated via Restricted Maximum Likelihood (REML) in `lme4`.

### Task 7.1: Varying-Intercept Null Model (`m1_mlm`)

We specify a two-level varying-intercept model with no explanatory variables, partitioning total variance in $y_{ij}$ (`covid19_r` for UTLAD $i$ in Region $j$) into a national fixed intercept ($\beta_0$), a Region-level random intercept deviation ($u_{0j} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2_{u0})$), and a UTLAD-level residual ($e_{ij} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2_e)$):

$$
y_{ij} = \beta_0 + u_{0j} + e_{ij}
$$

```{r ch7_m1}
# Baseline intercept-only OLS for reference
m0_ols <- lm(covid19_r ~ 1, data = nsdf)

# Model 1: Varying-intercept null model by Region
m1_mlm <- lmer(covid19_r ~ 1 + (1 | Region), data = nsdf)
summary(m1_mlm)
```

We inspect the fixed intercept (`fixef`), the Region-level random effects (`ranef`), and the 95% profile confidence intervals (`confint`).

```{r ch7_m1_details}

confint(m1_mlm, level = 0.95)
```

### Task 7.3: Variance Partition Coefficient (VPC)

The **Variance Partition Coefficient (VPC)** measures the proportion of total unexplained variance in `covid19_r` attributable to differences *between* Regions (Level 2):

$$

$$

## Multilevel Modelling — Part 2: Varying Slopes (Chapter 8 Tasks)

Varying-intercept models assume that the effect (slope) of each explanatory variable is constant across all Regions. ### Task 8.1: Varying-Slope Model (`m3_mlm`)

First, we estimate a varying-slope model (`m3_mlm`) using our mean-centered predictors (`z_crowded_hou`, `z_elderly`, `z_ethnic`, `z_lt_illness`), where the slope of `z_lt_illness` varies across Regions (`(0 + z_lt_illness | Region)`) while the intercept is fixed:

$$
y_{ij} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 z_{\text{Crowded},ij} + \beta_2 z_{\text{Elderly},ij} + \beta_3 z_{\text{Ethnic},ij} + (\beta_4 + u_{4j}) z_{\text{LTIll},ij} + e_{ij}
$$

```{r ch8_m3}
m3_mlm <- lmer(
  covid19_r ~ z_crowded_hou + z_elderly + z_ethnic + z_lt_illness + (0 + z_lt_illness | Region),
```

### Task 8.3: Model Comparison & Discussion

We formally compare our multilevel specifications (`m1_mlm` Null Varying-Intercept, `m2_mlm_c` Varying-Intercept with Predictors, `m3_mlm` Varying-Slope Only, and `m4_mlm` Varying-Intercept + Varying-Slope) using Likelihood Ratio Tests (`anova` refitted with Maximum Likelihood).

```{r ch8_anova}

```

**Analysis and Discussion (Chapter 8):**

1. **Comparison of Varying-Slope (`m3_mlm`) vs. Varying-Intercept + Varying-Slope (`m4_mlm`)**:
   - **Which is better and why?** The varying-intercept and varying-slope model (`m4_mlm`, or the parsimonious varying-intercept model `m2_mlm_c`) vastly outperforms the varying-slope-only model (`m3_mlm`). Specifically, `m3_mlm` yields an AIC of **`r round(anova_res["m3_mlm", "AIC"], 1)`** (deviance = **`r round(anova_res["m3_mlm", "deviance"], 1)`**), whereas adding varying regional intercepts in `m4_mlm` lowers AIC dramatically to **`r round(anova_res["m4_mlm", "AIC"], 1)`** (deviance = **`r round(anova_res["m4_mlm", "deviance"], 1)`**, $p < 0.001$). As emphasised by @gelman2006data, forcing all regions to share a single baseline intercept (`m3_mlm`) while allowing only the slope to tilt severely misspecifies the large baseline differences in regional infection waves across England.
   - Comparing `m2_mlm_c` (varying-intercept) against `m4_mlm` (varying-intercept and varying-slope), adding the random slope and covariance parameters lowers deviance marginally, showing that while the relationship between long-term illness and COVID-19 exhibits regional heterogeneity (stronger positive slopes in northern industrial regions such as the *North West* and *Yorkshire and The Humber*), the dominant source of regional hierarchy is baseline intercept variation ($u_{0j}$).

2. **Varying Slope Estimates and Intercept-Slope Correlation**:
   - Across all UTLADs, the fixed average slope for `z_lt_illness` in `m4_mlm` is strongly positive ($\hat{\beta}_4 = `r round(fixef(m4_mlm)["z_lt_illness"], 2)`$), indicating that a 1 percentage point increase in long-term illness share is associated with roughly **`r round(fixef(m4_mlm)["z_lt_illness"] / 100, 2)`** additional COVID-19 cases per 1,000 residents on average.
   - The correlation between regional intercept residuals ($u_{0j}$) and regional slope residuals ($u_{4j}$) in `m4_mlm` captures how baseline regional wave intensity interacts with health deprivation sensitivity: northern regions (*North West*, *North East*) exhibit steeper positive slopes (where chronic health deprivation strongly amplifies local infection risk), whereas southern regions with lower prevalence (*South West*) exhibit flatter slopes.

2. **Appropriate Bandwidth Selection (Fixed vs. Adaptive Kernel)**:
   - The **Adaptive Bandwidth (`abw = `r round(abw, 3)``)** is theoretically and empirically superior to the Fixed Bandwidth (`fbw = `r round(fbw, 1)` km`) for England's UTLAD geography.
   - **Why?** English UTLADs vary drastically in physical area and centroid spacing: London comprises 32 compact boroughs within a ~25 km radius, and metropolitan regions (*Greater Manchester*, *Merseyside*, *West Midlands*, *Tyne and Wear*) also consist of tightly packed districts, whereas rural shire counties (*Cumbria*, *Northumberland*, *Cornwall*, *Devon*, *North Yorkshire*) span vast territories. The adaptive kernel dynamically expands in sparse rural areas and contracts in dense metropolitan cores, achieving a lower AIC (**`r round(ab_gwr$results$AICh, 1)`** vs. **`r round(fb_gwr$results$AICh, 1)`**) and higher Quasi-global $R^2$ (**`r round(r2_ab, 3)`** vs. **`r round(r2_fb, 3)`**).

## Spatio-Temporal Data Analysis & Modelling (Chapter 10 Tasks)

Cross-sectional multilevel and GWR models analyse a single temporal aggregation ("snapshot") and cannot capture how the pandemic's trajectory evolved over time or how spatial and temporal dependencies interact [@wikle2019spatio; @zammit2017frk]. In Chapter 10, we reshape `covid19_eng.gpkg` into a long space-time format, construct a formal `STIDF` object, visualise space-time patterns, and fit Fixed Rank Kriging (`FRK`) basis-function regression models.

### Spatio-Temporal Data Wrangling & `STIDF` Construction

We reshape the daily COVID-19 columns (`X2020.12.01` to `X2021.01.29`, covering the 60 days of Wave 3 across all 149 UTLADs = $8,940$ space-time observations) from time-wide to space-time long format (`covid19_spt`), parse dates via `lubridate::ymd()`, compute daily new cases per 100,000 residents (`n_covid19_r`) and running cumulative cases per 100,000 (`c_covid19_r`), and construct a `spacetime::STIDF` object [@pebesma2012spacetime].

```{r ch10_wrangling}
# Reshape Wave-3 daily cases (2020-12-01 to 2021-01-29) from wide to long format

  st_drop_geometry() %>%
  dplyr::select(
    objectid, ctyua19cd, ctyua19nm, Region, long, lat,
    Residents, crowded_hou, elderly, ethnic, lt_illness, imd_score,
    "X2020.12.01":"X2021.01.29"
  ) %>%
  pivot_longer(
    cols = starts_with("X20"),
    names_to = "raw_date",
    values_to = "daily_cases"
  ) %>%
  mutate(
    # Clean date string ("X2020.12.01" -> "2020-12-01") and parse with lubridate
    date = ymd(str_replace_all(str_remove(raw_date, "^X"), "\\.", "-")),
    daily_cases = pmax(0, daily_cases),
    day = day(date),
    week = isoweek(date),
    month = month(date),
    year = year(date),
    # Continuous study day index (1 to 60)
    time_index = as.numeric(date - min(date)) + 1
  ) %>%
  group_by(ctyua19cd) %>%
  arrange(date, .by_group = TRUE) %>%
  mutate(
    cum_cases_wave3 = cumsum(daily_cases),
    # Daily rate of new COVID-19 cases per 100,000 residents (non-negative integer count rate)
    n_covid19_r = pmax(0, round((daily_cases / Residents) * 100000)),
    # Cumulative Wave-3 cases per 100,000 residents
    c_covid19_r = round((cum_cases_wave3 / Residents) * 100000)

  ungroup()

  dplyr::select(ctyua19cd, geom) %>%
  right_join(covid_long, by = "ctyua19cd") %>%
  st_as_sf()

  sp = spat_pts,
```

### Task 10.2: Fitting Spatio-Temporal Models (`FRK` Basis Functions & Count GLMs)

To model `n_covid19_r` across space and time while accounting for spatio-temporal autocorrelation and overdispersion [@cressie2008fixed; @zammit2017frk], we construct Gaussian spatial radial basis functions (`FRK::auto_basis` with `nres = 1`) evaluated at all UTLA coordinates (`FRK::eval_basis`).

```{r ch10_frk_basis}
# 1. Construct Gaussian radial basis functions over UTLA coordinates
G <- auto_basis(
  data = sp::SpatialPoints(as.matrix(covid_long[, c("long", "lat")])),
  nres = 1,
  type = "Gaussian"
)

# 2. Evaluate basis functions at each space-time observation
S <- eval_basis(
  basis = G,
  s = as.matrix(covid_long[, c("long", "lat")])
) %>%
  as.matrix()

colnames(S) <- paste0("B", 1:ncol(S))
cat("Number of generated Gaussian spatial basis functions:", ncol(S), "\n")

# 3. Combine covariates and basis functions into the regression dataframe
reg_df <- cbind(covid_long, S) %>%
  dplyr::select(
    ctyua19nm, n_covid19_r, long, lat, time_index,
    crowded_hou, elderly, ethnic, lt_illness,
    starts_with("B")
  )
```

We now estimate five progressive spatio-temporal regression specifications:

1. **`lm_m` (OLS Linear Trend-Surface + Basis Functions)**: Assumes Gaussian errors.
2. **`poisson_m1` (Poisson GLM with Log Link)**: Models count rates via $y_{it} \sim \text{Poisson}(\exp(X_{it}\beta))$ and tests for overdispersion ($\text{Deviance} / \text{df}_{\text{residual}}$).
3. **`qpoisson_m1` (Quasi-Poisson GLM)**: Adjusts standard errors for overdispersion ($\text{Var}(Y) = \phi \mu$).
4. **`nb_m1` (Negative Binomial GLM)**: Relaxes the mean-variance equality via `MASS::glm.nb`.
5. **`nb_m2` (Negative Binomial GLM with Space-Time Interactions)**: Incorporates two-way interactions between longitude, latitude, and time (`(long + lat + time_index)^2`) alongside all contextual predictors and spatial basis functions.


### Analysis and Discussion (Chapter 10)

1. **Comparison of GWR and Spatio-Temporal (ST) Model Results Across Space**:
   - **Consistency of Contextual Effects**: Once space-time trends and FRK Gaussian basis functions (`B1`–`B9`) are controlled for in the Negative Binomial models (`nb_m1` and `nb_m2`), the global contextual predictors align closely with the dominant local patterns identified by GWR in Chapter 9: `crowded_hou`, `ethnic`, and `lt_illness` exhibit positive and statistically significant associations with COVID-19 infection risk ($p < 0.001$), whereas `elderly` displays a statistically significant negative coefficient ($p < 0.001$).
   - **How GWR and ST Capture Spatial Variation Differently**: GWR (Chapter 9) models spatial non-stationarity by allowing the slopes of `crowded_hou`, `elderly`, `ethnic`, and `lt_illness` to vary locally across the 149 UTLADs within a single cross-sectional time window. By contrast, the FRK Spatio-Temporal model (Chapter 10) estimates global partial effects for the demographic covariates while decomposing continuous spatial variation and spatial dependence into a linear combination of radial basis functions (`B1`–`B9`, nearly all of which are highly statistically significant at $p < 0.001$ in `nb_m1` and `nb_m2`) alongside explicit coordinate trends (`long`, `lat`) and space-time interactions.
   - Furthermore, the Poisson model exhibits severe overdispersion ($\text{Deviance}/\text{df} = `r round(disp_ratio, 1)` \gg 1$), which causes standard Poisson standard errors to be artificially small. Moving to the **Negative Binomial (`nb_m1` and `nb_m2`)** reduces residual deviance from **`r round(poisson_m1$deviance, 0)`** to **`r round(nb_m2$deviance, 0)`**, providing much more reliable statistical inference.

2. **Significant Variations Over Time and Space-Time Interactions**:
   - **Temporal Autocorrelation and Trend**: The exploratory analysis demonstrates strong positive first-order temporal autocorrelation ($r_{t, t-1} = `r round(lag_cor, 3)`$) and a clear 7-day seasonal reporting cycle (Figure 5.4), alongside an inverted-U wave trajectory that surged through December 2020 and peaked in early January 2021 before declining under national lockdown.
   - **Space-Time Interaction Terms (`nb_m2`)**: In `nb_m1`, the main effect of `time_index` is positive and highly statistically significant ($\hat{\beta}_{\text{time}} = `r round(coef(nb_m1)["time_index"], 4)`$, $p < 0.001$), confirming a significant upward temporal trend across the Wave-3 window. Even more revealingly, in `nb_m2`, the space-time interaction terms **`long:time_index`** and **`lat:time_index`** (as well as **`long:lat`**) are highly statistically significant:
     - The significant **`lat:time_index`** interaction captures the south-to-north diffusion of the Alpha variant during Wave 3: whereas early December 2020 cases were heavily concentrated in Kent, London, and the South East (lower latitude, higher longitude), infection rates accelerated northward and westward (`long:time_index` and `lat:time_index`) into the Midlands, North West, and Cumbria as time progressed through January 2021 (as visually confirmed by the weekly maps in Figure 5.1 and the Hovmöller diagram in Figure 5.3).
