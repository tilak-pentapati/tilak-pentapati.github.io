1 Introduction

The focal point of this report centres around the research question: “What are the key determinants impacting the implementation of green central heating in the districts of the UK?” This question is highly relevant in the context of rising environmental awareness, residential decarbonisation targets, and the transition toward a sustainable energy future. Domestic heating accounts for a substantial share of national greenhouse gas emissions, meaning that the adoption of green central heating systems—such as heat pumps, biomass boilers, solar thermal, and district or communal heat networks—stands as a crucial pillar in reducing residential carbon footprints and promoting energy-efficient practices.

Understanding the socio-economic, demographic, and housing-stock factors that shape the uptake of green central heating technologies provides actionable insight for both public policymakers and private-sector energy markets. Effective decarbonisation policy requires more than technological availability; it requires identifying how lifestyle patterns, housing tenure, dwelling structure, and local heating infrastructure interact across different local authorities.

  4. Correlation Analysis of Outcome Variable screens bivariate associations between Green_Central_Heating and all other continuous and categorical variables in the dataset (excluding the identifier District) using rank-based correlation (Table 1).
  5. Selection of Predictor Variables justifies the five chosen candidate predictors (four continuous and one newly constructed binary categorical variable, Age_Profile).

2 Literature Review and Variables Selection Rationale

Recent studies in environmental sciences and energy socio-economics have investigated the drivers behind household adoption of sustainable technologies such as green central heating. Empirical research demonstrates that residential technology adoption is not merely a function of technological availability, but is strongly conditioned by socio-economic and demographic characteristics (Hooks et al., 2022). Educational attainment, economic activity, housing type, tenure, and environmental awareness play central roles in the uptake of green energy technologies, as highlighted by Bruckmann, Willibald and Blanco (2021) in their regional study of sustainable technology diffusion without strong policy mandates.

Drawing on this literature, potential district-level determinants include economic activity and employment structure (Full_Time_Employee), dwelling morphology (Detached), household tenure (Owned), demographic age structure (Age_Profile), and existing heating infrastructure (No_Central_Heating). In particular, examining the proportion of households with no central heating (No_Central_Heating) allows us to test whether off-grid districts lacking conventional mains-gas central heating are more inclined to adopt alternative green central heating options, or whether a lack of central heating instead reflects urban deprivation and lower capacity for capital-intensive energy retrofits.

3.3 Analytical Workflow

Our modelling workflow proceeds in five stages:

  1. Univariate exploration of the continuous outcome variable Green_Central_Heating to assess its distributional shape and select appropriate summary statistics.
  2. Bivariate correlation screening across all candidate variables in the dataset (excluding the identifier District), alongside the creation of a recoded binary demographic factor Age_Profile (dichotomising Age_65_plus at its median into "Youthful" vs. "Ageing" districts).
  3. Narrative regression modelling starting from a single focal predictor (Full_Time_Employee in model1), adding housing and heating controls (Detached, No_Central_Heating, Owned in model2), and testing the demographic factor (Age_Profile in model3) to identify our initial ‘best’ model.
  4. Refinement via interactions and polynomials, first testing all \binom{4}{2} = 6 two-way interactions (model2i_first) and eliminating non-significant terms to obtain model2i (Model 2 IS), then testing quadratic polynomial terms (M1–M4) to arrive at the final model M2.
  5. Model diagnostics and predictor adequacy, evaluating residual normality, constant error variance, multicollinearity, autocorrelation, and single-predictor R^2 adequacy (adequacy_lm()).

4 Univariate Description of Outcome Variable

Our outcome variable of interest is Green_Central_Heating, a continuous variable measuring the percentage of households in a local authority district that use one or more ‘green’ forms of central heating (defined in the Census 2021 data dictionary as renewable energy systems such as heat pumps or biomass, district/communal heat networks, or tank/bottled gas).

4.1 Skewness, Central Tendency, and Dispersion

Before summarising a continuous variable, we examine its skewness, which measures the asymmetry of its distribution around its mean \bar{Y}:

\text{Skewness} = \frac{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(Y_i - \bar{Y})^3}{\left[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(Y_i - \bar{Y})^2\right]^{3/2}}

A symmetric normal distribution has \text{Skewness} \approx 0, whereas distributions with |\text{Skewness}| > 1 are substantially skewed. When a continuous variable has \text{Skewness} > 1, the arithmetic mean and standard deviation are distorted by extreme values in the right tail. We therefore use the Median (50\text{th} percentile, Q_2) to measure central tendency and the Inter-Quartile Range (\text{IQR} = Q_3 - Q_1) to measure dispersion across the middle 50\% of districts.

5 Correlation Analysis of Outcome Variable

To satisfy Instruction #5 (“Examine how strongly correlated your outcome variable is with ALL of the other variables in the dataset”) and prepare a categorical demographic predictor for Instruction #6, we create a working copy of the dataset (census_copy) and construct a binary categorical variable, Age_Profile, by splitting districts at the median percentage of residents aged 65 and over (Age_65_plus):

  • Districts with Age_65_plus < median(Age_65_plus) are classified as "Youthful".
  • Districts with Age_65_plus >= median(Age_65_plus) are classified as "Ageing".

5.1 Choice of Correlation Measure and Treatment of Variable Types

Following the course correlation guidelines (Assignment-2--correlations.html):

  1. Exclusion of Identifier Variables: District is a unique nominal identifier for each local authority (1 to 331 in alphabetical order) rather than a substantive characteristic, so it is excluded from the correlation analysis (ID_variables <- "District").
  2. Continuous Predictors (48 variables): Because the continuous outcome Green_Central_Heating is positively skewed (\text{skew} = 1.73) and several continuous predictors also exhibit skewness, Spearman’s rank correlation (\rho, method = "spearman") is the primary non-parametric measure of monotonic association, supplemented alongside Pearson’s linear correlation (r, method = "pearson"): r_{xy} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})}{\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i - \bar{x})^2 \sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_i - \bar{y})^2}}
  3. Categorical / Nominal Predictors (6 variables: County, Region, Super_Region, South_East, Country, and Age_Profile): Because the outcome variable is continuous, Cramér’s V cannot be computed. Converting factor variables to numeric codes (as.numeric) allows Spearman’s \rho (and Pearson’s r) to be calculated via cor.results(). For binary variables (South_East and Age_Profile), the magnitude of the correlation is invariant to category order; for multi-category nominal variables (County, Region, Super_Region, Country), the coefficient depends on factor category ordering and serves only as a rough guide.

7 Regression Model Fitting Strategy & Initial ‘Best’ Model Identification

7.1 OLS Multiple Regression and Model-Fitting Strategy

For our continuous outcome variable Y_i (Green_Central_Heating), an Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) multiple linear regression model with k predictors takes the form:

Y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X_{1i} + \beta_2 X_{2i} + \dots + \beta_k X_{ki} + \varepsilon_i, \quad \varepsilon_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)

where \beta_0 is the intercept, each partial regression coefficient \beta_j measures the expected change in Y for a one-unit increase in X_j holding all other predictors constant, and \varepsilon_i = Y_i - \hat{Y}_i is the residual error. We evaluate model fit using the Coefficient of Determination (R^2 = 1 - \text{SSR}/\text{SST}), Adjusted R^2, and the Akaike Information Criterion (\text{AIC} = 2p - 2\ln\hat{L}), where lower \text{AIC} values indicate a superior balance between goodness of fit and model parsimony.

Among the four regression model-fitting strategies covered in ENVS450 (Narrative, Backwards elimination, Backward stepwise, and All subsets), we explicitly adopt the Narrative approach. Starting with a baseline model containing our focal socio-economic predictor Full_Time_Employee (Model 1), we sequentially introduce housing and heating infrastructure controls—Detached, No_Central_Heating, and Owned (Model 2)—followed by the demographic factor Age_Profile (Model 3). This allows us to test how much of the initial bivariate effect of Full_Time_Employee is “explained away” by confounding housing and demographic differences across districts, stopping when adding further predictors yields negligible substantive gain in explanatory power (R^2) relative to added model complexity.

8 Interaction Effect Evaluation

8.1 Multiplicative Interaction Effects

In an additive linear model (Model 2), the partial slope of each predictor X_1 is assumed to be constant across all levels of the other predictors. Adding a two-way multiplicative interaction term \beta_{12}(X_1 \cdot X_2) allows the marginal effect of X_1 on \hat{Y} to depend linearly on the moderator X_2:

\frac{\partial \hat{Y}}{\partial X_1} = \beta_1 + \beta_{12} X_2

Here, the main-effect coefficient \beta_1 represents the conditional slope of X_1 specifically when X_2 = 0, while \beta_{12} measures how much the slope of X_1 changes for each one-unit increase in X_2.

### Skewness, Central Tendency, and Dispersion

Before summarising a continuous variable, we examine its **skewness**, which measures the asymmetry of its distribution around its mean $\bar{Y}$:

$$\text{Skewness} = \frac{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(Y_i - \bar{Y})^3}{\left[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(Y_i - \bar{Y})^2\right]^{3/2}}$$

A symmetric normal distribution has $\text{Skewness} \approx 0$, whereas distributions with $|\text{Skewness}| > 1$ are substantially skewed. When a continuous variable has $\text{Skewness} > 1$, the arithmetic mean and standard deviation are distorted by extreme values in the right tail. We therefore use the **Median** ($50\text{th}$ percentile, $Q_2$) to measure central tendency and the **Inter-Quartile Range ($\text{IQR} = Q_3 - Q_1$)** to measure dispersion across the middle $50\%$ of districts.

## Regression Model Fitting Strategy & Initial 'Best' Model Identification

### OLS Multiple Regression and Model-Fitting Strategy

For our continuous outcome variable $Y_i$ (`Green_Central_Heating`), an Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) multiple linear regression model with $k$ predictors takes the form:

$$Y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X_{1i} + \beta_2 X_{2i} + \dots + \beta_k X_{ki} + \varepsilon_i, \quad \varepsilon_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)$$

where $\beta_0$ is the intercept, each partial regression coefficient $\beta_j$ measures the expected change in $Y$ for a one-unit increase in $X_j$ holding all other predictors constant, and $\varepsilon_i = Y_i - \hat{Y}_i$ is the residual error. We evaluate model fit using the **Coefficient of Determination ($R^2 = 1 - \text{SSR}/\text{SST}$)**, **Adjusted $R^2$**, and the **Akaike Information Criterion ($\text{AIC} = 2p - 2\ln\hat{L}$)**, where lower $\text{AIC}$ values indicate a superior balance between goodness of fit and model parsimony.

Among the four regression model-fitting strategies covered in **ENVS450** (*Narrative*, *Backwards elimination*, *Backward stepwise*, and *All subsets*), we explicitly adopt the **Narrative approach**. Starting with a baseline model containing our focal socio-economic predictor `Full_Time_Employee` (**Model 1**), we sequentially introduce housing and heating infrastructure controls—`Detached`, `No_Central_Heating`, and `Owned` (**Model 2**)—followed by the demographic factor `Age_Profile` (**Model 3**). This allows us to test how much of the initial bivariate effect of `Full_Time_Employee` is **"explained away"** by confounding housing and demographic differences across districts, stopping when adding further predictors yields negligible substantive gain in explanatory power ($R^2$) relative to added model complexity.

## Interaction Effect Evaluation

### Multiplicative Interaction Effects

In an additive linear model (`Model 2`), the partial slope of each predictor $X_1$ is assumed to be constant across all levels of the other predictors. Adding a two-way multiplicative interaction term $\beta_{12}(X_1 \cdot X_2)$ allows the marginal effect of $X_1$ on $\hat{Y}$ to depend linearly on the moderator $X_2$:

$$\frac{\partial \hat{Y}}{\partial X_1} = \beta_1 + \beta_{12} X_2$$

Here, the main-effect coefficient $\beta_1$ represents the conditional slope of $X_1$ specifically when $X_2 = 0$, while $\beta_{12}$ measures how much the slope of $X_1$ changes for each one-unit increase in $X_2$.

&\quad - 2.7318(\text{No\_Central\_Heating}) - 0.1879(\text{Owned}) \\
&\quad + 0.0458(\text{Detached} \times \text{No\_Central\_Heating}) \\
&\quad + 0.0353(\text{No\_Central\_Heating} \times \text{Owned}) \\
&\quad + 0.0037(\text{Detached} \times \text{Owned})
\end{aligned}$$

- **Intercept (`21.8933 **`):** The predicted usage of green central heating is $21.89\%$ when all predictors equal zero.
- **Conditional Main Effects vs. Positive Interactions:** When `Detached = 0` and `Owned = 0`, a $1$ percentage point increase in `No_Central_Heating` changes predicted `Green_Central_Heating` by $-2.7318$ percentage points. However, because both `Detached:No_Central_Heating` ($+0.0458^{**}$) and `No_Central_Heating:Owned` ($+0.0353^{**}$) are positive, the marginal effect of `No_Central_Heating`:
  $$\frac{\partial \widehat{\text{GCH}}}{\partial \text{No\_Central\_Heating}} = -2.7318 + 0.0458(\text{Detached}) + 0.0353(\text{Owned})$$
  becomes strongly **positive** in districts with typical or high shares of detached and owner-occupied housing. We visualise these marginal effects across the observed ranges of the predictors in **Figures 1–3**.

### 10.2 Homoscedasticity (Constant Error Variance — Figure 5 & `ncvTest`)

We test the assumption of constant error variance ($\text{Var}(\varepsilon_i) = \sigma^2$) visually via `car::spreadLevelPlot(M2)` (**Figure 5**) and formally via the Breusch-Pagan / Cook-Weisberg Non-Constant Variance Score Test (`car::ncvTest(M2)`), where the **null hypothesis ($H_0$)** is **constant error variance (homoscedasticity)** and the alternative hypothesis ($H_1$) is that error variance changes systematically with the fitted values (**heteroscedasticity**):

```{r}

#| code-summary: "Plot Figure 5: spreadLevelPlot(M2) and run ncvTest(M2)"

spreadLevelPlot(M2)
```

```{r}
#| label: ncv-test-m2
#| code-summary: "Run Non-Constant Variance Score Test ncvTest(M2)"

ncvTest(M2)
```

**Interpretation of Figure 5 and `ncvTest(M2)`:**
In **Figure 5**, the dashed reference line slopes upward from left to right and the solid magenta smoother curves across the left and middle of the plot, indicating that the spread of the studentized residuals increases as the predicted values of `Green_Central_Heating` increase. Formally, `ncvTest(M2)` yields $\chi^2 = 38.16122$ ($\text{df} = 1$) with **$p = 6.5134 \times 10^{-10} \ll 0.05$**. Because $p < 0.05$, we **reject the null hypothesis of constant error variance** and conclude that the model exhibits statistically significant **heteroscedasticity** (error variance is larger in high-adoption rural districts than in low-adoption urban districts).

**How this breach of homoscedasticity can be addressed (Instruction #12b):**

1. **Power / Box-Cox Transformation of the Outcome Variable:** `spreadLevelPlot(M2)` suggests a stabilising power transformation of $\lambda = -1.066471$ (approximately an inverse transformation $Y^{-1}$, or a logarithmic transformation $\log(Y)$) to compress the right tail and stabilise error variance.
2. **Heteroscedasticity-Consistent (HC3) Robust Standard Errors:** Computing White's heteroscedasticity-robust standard errors (`car::hccm(M2)`) provides valid $t$-tests and confidence intervals without altering the original percentage scale of `Green_Central_Heating`.
3. **Weighted Least Squares (WLS):** Weighting districts inversely to their estimated error variance.

### 10.5 Real-World Interpretation: What the Model Reveals About the UK (Instruction #12d)

Our final model (`M2`, explaining **$58.52\%$** of the district-level variation across England and Wales) reveals four key insights into the real-world geography of residential green central heating in 2021:

1. **The Rural & Off-Gas-Grid Housing Effect (`Detached`, `I(Detached^2)`, and `No_Central_Heating`):**
   Dwelling morphology (`Detached` and `I(Detached^2)`) is the single strongest determinant of green central heating adoption ($54.03\%$ adequacy). Moreover, the positive interaction between `Detached` and `No_Central_Heating` (**Figure 2**) shows that green central heating uptake is concentrated in rural, low-density districts where detached homes are off the UK's mains natural-gas grid. In urban districts dominated by flats and terraced housing, a lack of central heating does not lead to green central heating adoption; in rural detached-housing districts off the gas grid, households rely heavily on renewable heat pumps, biomass boilers, communal heat networks, or tank/bottled gas.
2. **The Enabling Role of Homeownership (`Owned` Interactions):**
   While `Owned` has a modest standalone correlation ($r = 0.191$, adequacy $= 6.24\%$), its positive interactions with `Detached` (**Figure 1**, crossover at $36.5\%$ detached) and `No_Central_Heating` highlight a critical tenure divide: in districts with detached, off-grid housing stock, **owner-occupation substantially accelerates green heating adoption**. Homeowners have the property rights and long-term financial horizon required to invest in alternative heating systems, whereas private and social rented sectors face split-incentive barriers.
3. **Urban Labour Markets vs. Rural Demographics (`Full_Time_Employee` and `Age_Profile`):**
   `Full_Time_Employee` is negatively associated with `Green_Central_Heating` ($\beta = -0.0806^{**}$, **Figure 3**). However, our Narrative modelling (**Table 2**) demonstrated that nearly half of the initial bivariate effect of `Full_Time_Employee` is explained away once we control for housing morphology (`Detached`), heating infrastructure (`No_Central_Heating`), tenure (`Owned`), and demographic ageing (`Age_Profile`). Urban commuter districts with younger, full-time working populations are overwhelmingly connected to the mains gas grid, whereas older, rural districts lead in non-mains/green central heating adoption.
4. **Policy Implications:**
   To achieve broad residential decarbonisation across the UK, climate and energy policy cannot rely solely on off-grid rural homeowners installing heat pumps or biomass in detached houses. Policymakers must address the **urban mains-gas and rented housing sectors**—expanding district heat networks for high-density flats, providing targeted grants for lower-income urban authorities, and introducing regulatory incentives to overcome landlord-tenant split incentives.
