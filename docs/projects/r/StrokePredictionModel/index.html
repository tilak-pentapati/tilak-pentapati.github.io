1 Project Overview

According to the World Health Organization (WHO), stroke is the second leading cause of mortality globally, responsible for approximately 11\% of total deaths worldwide (over 6.6 million deaths annually) [1], [2]. Characterized by an acute focal neurological deficit resulting from vascular injury to the central nervous system—primarily ischemic cerebral infarction (87\%) or intracranial hemorrhage (13\%)—stroke is also a preeminent driver of chronic disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) [3].

Epidemiological evidence indicates that up to 80\% of premature strokes are preventable through early detection, clinical risk stratification, and targeted management of modifiable vascular risk factors, including hypertension, hyperglycemia, dyslipidemia, and smoking [4], [5].

While traditional clinical risk tools like the Framingham Stroke Risk Profile (FSRP) rely on linear cox-proportional hazard regressions with fixed risk factors [6], modern machine learning (ML) architectures trained on Electronic Health Record (EHR) data offer opportunities to capture complex, non-linear interactions across multifaceted patient histories [7], [8].

3 Part 1: Data Cleaning, Factor Encoding & Missingness Mechanics

3.1 Factor Theory and Type Coercion in R

In statistical computing, transforming raw categorical data into R factor data structures is essential for both computational performance and statistical validity:

  1. Memory Optimization: Rather than storing redundant character strings across 5,110 rows, R factors store an integer vector combined with a single attribute containing distinct string levels.
  2. Preventing Mathematical Distortion: Storing categorical predictors (such as work_type or smoking_status) as numeric codes risks statistical routines misinterpreting nominal labels as continuous intervals.
  3. Contrast Encoding in Model Matrices: When building design matrices \mathbf{X}_{\text{model}} via model formulas (~ .), R dynamically converts K-level factors into K - 1 orthogonal dummy indicator variables relative to an established reference level [7].

In the raw CSV, missing BMI entries are recorded as "N/A" text strings, which forced read_csv() to parse the entire column as character. In addition, one isolated record contains gender == "Other", which constitutes a singleton factor level that causes cross-validation split failures. We resolve these issues below:

Code 
stroke_clean <- stroke_raw %>%
  # Remove administrative ID to eliminate target leakage
  select(-id) %>%
  mutate(
    # Recode "N/A" strings to true NA and cast to numeric
    bmi = na_if(bmi, "N/A"),
    bmi = as.numeric(bmi),
    
    # Recode singleton "Other" gender to NA
    gender = if_else(gender == "Other", NA_character_, gender),
    
    # Coerce categorical descriptors to factors
    gender = factor(gender),
    hypertension = factor(hypertension, levels = c(0, 1), labels = c("No", "Yes")),
    heart_disease = factor(heart_disease, levels = c(0, 1), labels = c("No", "Yes")),
    ever_married = factor(ever_married),
    work_type = factor(work_type),
    Residence_type = factor(Residence_type),
    smoking_status = factor(smoking_status),
    
    # Target variable: stroke (0 = No, 1 = Yes)
    stroke = factor(stroke, levels = c(0, 1), labels = c("No", "Yes"))
  ) %>%
  filter(!is.na(gender)) # Exclude singleton gender row

3.2 Missing Data Taxonomy: Imputation vs. Informative Missingness

Missing data represents a critical challenge in clinical biostatistics. According to Little and Rubin’s taxonomy [9], missingness falls into three classes:

  1. Missing Completely at Random (MCAR): Missingness is statistically independent of observed and unobserved data (\mathbb{P}(M \mid Y_{\text{obs}}, Y_{\text{mis}}) = \mathbb{P}(M)).
  2. Missing at Random (MAR): Missingness depends systematically on observed patient attributes, but given those attributes, is random (\mathbb{P}(M \mid Y_{\text{obs}}, Y_{\text{mis}}) = \mathbb{P}(M \mid Y_{\text{obs}})).
  3. Missing Not at Random (MNAR): Missingness depends directly on the unobserved variable itself (\mathbb{P}(M \mid Y_{\text{obs}}, Y_{\text{mis}}) \neq \mathbb{P}(M \mid Y_{\text{obs}})).

For bmi, exactly 201 values (3.93\%) are missing. Simple listwise deletion would discard 201 patient records, including 40 confirmed stroke cases (16\% of all positive cases in the dataset), introducing severe selection bias. Because BMI displays a pronounced positive skew (\text{Skewness} > 1.0), we impute missing values via the conditional median stratified by sex and age cohort.

Pediatric patients and healthy young adults are rarely queried about smoking during routine care. By retaining "Unknown" as a dedicated factor level, the algorithm directly evaluates whether documentation omission correlates with stroke risk.

Code 
# Conditional median imputation for BMI stratified by age deciles and biological sex
stroke_imputed <- stroke_clean %>%
  mutate(age_decile = cut(age, breaks = seq(0, 90, by = 10), include.lowest = TRUE)) %>%
  group_by(gender, age_decile) %>%
  mutate(bmi = if_else(is.na(bmi), median(bmi, na.rm = TRUE), bmi)) %>%
  ungroup() %>%
  # Fallback to global median if an age group is sparse
  mutate(bmi = if_else(is.na(bmi), median(bmi, na.rm = TRUE), bmi)) %>%
  select(-age_decile)

5 Part 3: Clinical Feature Engineering

Informed by clinical practice guidelines [3], [12], we engineer three domain-specific biomarkers:

  1. glucose_tier (ADA Criteria):
    • Normal: < 100 \text{ mg/dL}
    • Prediabetes: 100 - 125 \text{ mg/dL}
    • Diabetes: \ge 126 \text{ mg/dL}
  2. bmi_tier (WHO Criteria):
    • Underweight: < 18.5
    • Normal: 18.5 - 24.9
    • Overweight: 25.0 - 29.9
    • Obese: \ge 30.0
  3. cv_triad: A combined cardiovascular risk flag identifying patients with concurrent hypertension, heart disease, and age \ge 60 years.
Code 
stroke_features <- stroke_imputed %>%
  mutate(
    # Clinical glucose stratification
    glucose_tier = case_when(
      avg_glucose_level < 100 ~ "Normal",
      avg_glucose_level < 126 ~ "Prediabetes",
      TRUE ~ "Diabetes"
    ) %>% factor(levels = c("Normal", "Prediabetes", "Diabetes")),
    
    # WHO BMI stratification
    bmi_tier = case_when(
      bmi < 18.5 ~ "Underweight",
      bmi < 25.0 ~ "Normal",
      bmi < 30.0 ~ "Overweight",
      TRUE ~ "Obese"
    ) %>% factor(levels = c("Underweight", "Normal", "Overweight", "Obese")),
    
    # Cardiovascular Triad risk flag
    cv_triad = if_else(
      hypertension == "Yes" & heart_disease == "Yes" & age >= 60,
      "High_Risk", 
      "Standard"
    ) %>% factor(levels = c("Standard", "High_Risk"))
  )

6 Part 4: Imbalanced Learning & Tidymodels Pipeline (SMOTE)

6.1 The Accuracy Paradox

In this cohort, 4,860 out of 5,109 patients (95.13\%) are stroke-free. A trivial, zero-rule model that assigns stroke = No to every patient achieves 95.13\% accuracy while possessing a Sensitivity of 0\% [7], [13].

Because the clinical cost of a False Negative vastly exceeds that of a False Positive (C_{FN} \gg C_{FP}), models must be formulated to optimize Sensitivity and AUC-ROC rather than raw accuracy [14].

6.2 Synthetic Minority Over-sampling (SMOTE)

We employ SMOTE via the themis package [15]. Rather than duplicating minority records, SMOTE synthesizes novel minority cases by identifying the K nearest neighbors for each minority instance \mathbf{x}_i \in S_{\text{min}} and interpolating in feature space:

\mathbf{x}_{\text{synthetic}} = \mathbf{x}_i + \lambda (\mathbf{x}_{zi} - \mathbf{x}_i), \quad \lambda \sim U(0, 1)

# Generate predicted class probabilities and class labels on test set
eval_model <- function(fitted_wf, model_label, test_df, thresh = 0.50) {
  probs <- predict(fitted_wf, new_data = test_df, type = "prob")
  
  df_res <- test_df %>%
    select(stroke) %>%
    bind_cols(probs) %>%
    mutate(
      .pred_class = factor(if_else(.pred_Yes >= thresh, "Yes", "No"), levels = c("No", "Yes")),
      Model = model_label,
      Threshold = thresh
    )
  
  # Calculate metrics using yardstick with event_level = "second" for "Yes"
  sens <- sens(df_res, truth = stroke, estimate = .pred_class, event_level = "second")$.estimate

8 Part 6: Clinical Decision Threshold Calibration (Youden’s J)

In clinical risk screening, setting the decision threshold to p = 0.50 is rarely optimal because event prevalence is low (4.9\%). Using the pROC package, we determine the optimal operating threshold via Youden’s J statistic, which maximizes the trade-off between sensitivity and specificity [14]:

J = \max_p (\text{Sensitivity}(p) + \text{Specificity}(p) - 1)

function score(x) {
  const parts = terms.map(t => {
    const raw = t.kind === "numeric" ? x[t.variable] : (x[t.variable] === t.level ? 1 : 0);
    return {term: t.term, contribution: t.coef * (raw - t.mean) / t.sd};
  });
  const eta = meta.intercept + d3.sum(parts, d => d.contribution);
  return {eta, p: 1 / (1 + Math.exp(-eta)), parts};
}

Why Observable JS rather than Shinylive? A logistic regression is a dot product plus a sigmoid, so the browser can reproduce the fitted model to floating-point precision (checked above) with a few kilobytes of exported numbers. Shinylive would download a WebAssembly R runtime and the tidymodels stack (tens of megabytes) to compute the same number; it becomes the right tool only for models that cannot be reduced to exported parameters, such as the random forest or XGBoost fits.

10 Part 8: Epidemiological Findings & Methodological Takeaways

10.1 Key Clinical Findings

  1. Age as the Primary Non-Modifiable Determinant: Consistent with the Global Burden of Disease Stroke Study [2], patient age is the single most powerful predictor of cerebrovascular events. Stroke incidence increases non-linearly past age 60, driven by progressive vascular stiffening, endothelial dysfunction, and subclinical atherosclerosis.
  2. Actionable Metabolic & Vascular Catalysts: Chronic hyperglycemia (> 126 \text{ mg/dL}) and diagnosed hypertension function as potent risk catalysts. Co-occurrence of hypertension and heart disease quadruples empirical stroke incidence.
  3. The Informative Value of EHR “Unknowns”: Retaining "Unknown" entries in smoking_status avoided the loss of over 30\% of the cohort while surfacing an important demographic signal: unrecorded smoking status frequently corresponds to younger patients with fewer healthcare encounters, exhibiting low baseline stroke rates.

10.2 Methodological Lessons in Medical Machine Learning

  • Abandoning Raw Classification Accuracy: In rare-event clinical epidemiology (4.9\% prevalence), standard accuracy is fundamentally flawed. Relying on default 0.50 thresholds in unweighted models yielded 95.2\% accuracy but missed 100\% of actual stroke events.
  • The Value of Synthetic Resampling (SMOTE): Incorporating SMOTE within a unified tidymodels pipeline elevated model sensitivity from 0.0\% to 65.3\% (both at p = 0.50), enabling the classifier to capture subtle physiological signals across the minority class.
  • Explainability vs. Ensembles: Random Forest and XGBoost reached \text{AUC} = 0.79 and 0.79; SMOTE-balanced Logistic Regression discriminated better (\text{AUC} = 0.82) while providing interpretable odds ratios that clinicians can review and understand at the bedside.

## Project Overview

According to the **World Health Organization (WHO)**, stroke is the second leading cause of mortality globally, responsible for approximately $11\%$ of total deaths worldwide (over 6.6 million deaths annually) [@who2020stroke; @feigin2021global]. Characterized by an acute focal neurological deficit resulting from vascular injury to the central nervous system—primarily ischemic cerebral infarction ($87\%$) or intracranial hemorrhage ($13\%$)—stroke is also a preeminent driver of chronic disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) [@boehme2017stroke].

Epidemiological evidence indicates that up to **$80\%$ of premature strokes are preventable** through early detection, clinical risk stratification, and targeted management of modifiable vascular risk factors, including hypertension, hyperglycemia, dyslipidemia, and smoking [@goldstein2011primary; @wolf1991probability]. 

While traditional clinical risk tools like the **Framingham Stroke Risk Profile (FSRP)** rely on linear cox-proportional hazard regressions with fixed risk factors [@d1994stroke], modern machine learning (ML) architectures trained on Electronic Health Record (EHR) data offer opportunities to capture complex, non-linear interactions across multifaceted patient histories [@kuhn2013applied; @steyerberg2010assessing].

## Part 1: Data Cleaning, Factor Encoding & Missingness Mechanics

### Factor Theory and Type Coercion in R

In statistical computing, transforming raw categorical data into R `factor` data structures is essential for both computational performance and statistical validity:

1. **Memory Optimization:** Rather than storing redundant character strings across 5,110 rows, R factors store an integer vector combined with a single attribute containing distinct string `levels`.
2. **Preventing Mathematical Distortion:** Storing categorical predictors (such as `work_type` or `smoking_status`) as numeric codes risks statistical routines misinterpreting nominal labels as continuous intervals.
3. **Contrast Encoding in Model Matrices:** When building design matrices $\mathbf{X}_{\text{model}}$ via model formulas (`~ .`), R dynamically converts $K$-level factors into $K - 1$ orthogonal dummy indicator variables relative to an established reference level [@kuhn2013applied].

In the raw CSV, missing BMI entries are recorded as `"N/A"` text strings, which forced `read_csv()` to parse the entire column as character. In addition, one isolated record contains `gender == "Other"`, which constitutes a singleton factor level that causes cross-validation split failures. We resolve these issues below:

```{r clean-types}
stroke_clean <- stroke_raw %>%
  # Remove administrative ID to eliminate target leakage
  select(-id) %>%
  mutate(
    # Recode "N/A" strings to true NA and cast to numeric
    bmi = na_if(bmi, "N/A"),
    bmi = as.numeric(bmi),
    
    # Recode singleton "Other" gender to NA
    gender = if_else(gender == "Other", NA_character_, gender),
    
    # Coerce categorical descriptors to factors
    gender = factor(gender),
    hypertension = factor(hypertension, levels = c(0, 1), labels = c("No", "Yes")),
    heart_disease = factor(heart_disease, levels = c(0, 1), labels = c("No", "Yes")),
    ever_married = factor(ever_married),
    work_type = factor(work_type),
    Residence_type = factor(Residence_type),
    smoking_status = factor(smoking_status),
    
    # Target variable: stroke (0 = No, 1 = Yes)
    stroke = factor(stroke, levels = c(0, 1), labels = c("No", "Yes"))
  ) %>%
  filter(!is.na(gender)) # Exclude singleton gender row
```

### Missing Data Taxonomy: Imputation vs. Informative Missingness

Missing data represents a critical challenge in clinical biostatistics. According to Little and Rubin's taxonomy [@little2019statistical], missingness falls into three classes:

1. **Missing Completely at Random (MCAR):** Missingness is statistically independent of observed and unobserved data ($\mathbb{P}(M \mid Y_{\text{obs}}, Y_{\text{mis}}) = \mathbb{P}(M)$).
2. **Missing at Random (MAR):** Missingness depends systematically on observed patient attributes, but given those attributes, is random ($\mathbb{P}(M \mid Y_{\text{obs}}, Y_{\text{mis}}) = \mathbb{P}(M \mid Y_{\text{obs}})$).
3. **Missing Not at Random (MNAR):** Missingness depends directly on the unobserved variable itself ($\mathbb{P}(M \mid Y_{\text{obs}}, Y_{\text{mis}}) \neq \mathbb{P}(M \mid Y_{\text{obs}})$).

```{r audit-missingness}
missing_audit <- stroke_clean %>%
  summarise(across(everything(), ~ sum(is.na(.)))) %>%
  pivot_longer(everything(), names_to = "Variable", values_to = "Missing_Count") %>%

    Total_Observations = nrow(stroke_clean),
    Missing_Percentage = round((Missing_Count / Total_Observations) * 100, 2)

      caption = "Table 1: Quantitative Audit of Missing Clinical Values") %>%

```

For `bmi`, exactly $201$ values ($3.93\%$) are missing. Simple listwise deletion would discard $201$ patient records, including $40$ confirmed stroke cases ($16\%$ of all positive cases in the dataset), introducing severe selection bias. Because BMI displays a pronounced positive skew ($\text{Skewness} > 1.0$), we impute missing values via the **conditional median stratified by sex and age cohort**.

In `smoking_status`, $1,544$ records ($30.22\%$) are labeled `"Unknown"`. In medical informatics, **missingness in electronic health records is rarely random—it is informative** [@groenwold2020informative; @sterne2009multiple]. Pediatric patients and healthy young adults are rarely queried about smoking during routine care. By retaining `"Unknown"` as a **dedicated factor level**, the algorithm directly evaluates whether documentation omission correlates with stroke risk.

```{r impute-bmi}
# Conditional median imputation for BMI stratified by age deciles and biological sex
stroke_imputed <- stroke_clean %>%
  mutate(age_decile = cut(age, breaks = seq(0, 90, by = 10), include.lowest = TRUE)) %>%
  group_by(gender, age_decile) %>%
  mutate(bmi = if_else(is.na(bmi), median(bmi, na.rm = TRUE), bmi)) %>%
  ungroup() %>%
  # Fallback to global median if an age group is sparse
  mutate(bmi = if_else(is.na(bmi), median(bmi, na.rm = TRUE), bmi)) %>%
  select(-age_decile)
```

## Part 3: Clinical Feature Engineering

Informed by clinical practice guidelines [@ada2023standards; @boehme2017stroke], we engineer three domain-specific biomarkers:

1. **`glucose_tier` (ADA Criteria):**
   - *Normal:* $< 100 \text{ mg/dL}$
   - *Prediabetes:* $100 - 125 \text{ mg/dL}$
   - *Diabetes:* $\ge 126 \text{ mg/dL}$
2. **`bmi_tier` (WHO Criteria):**
   - *Underweight:* $< 18.5$
   - *Normal:* $18.5 - 24.9$
   - *Overweight:* $25.0 - 29.9$
   - *Obese:* $\ge 30.0$
3. **`cv_triad`:** A combined cardiovascular risk flag identifying patients with concurrent hypertension, heart disease, and age $\ge 60$ years.

```{r feature-engineering}
stroke_features <- stroke_imputed %>%
  mutate(
    # Clinical glucose stratification
    glucose_tier = case_when(
      avg_glucose_level < 100 ~ "Normal",
      avg_glucose_level < 126 ~ "Prediabetes",
      TRUE ~ "Diabetes"
    ) %>% factor(levels = c("Normal", "Prediabetes", "Diabetes")),
    
    # WHO BMI stratification
    bmi_tier = case_when(
      bmi < 18.5 ~ "Underweight",
      bmi < 25.0 ~ "Normal",
      bmi < 30.0 ~ "Overweight",
      TRUE ~ "Obese"
    ) %>% factor(levels = c("Underweight", "Normal", "Overweight", "Obese")),
    
    # Cardiovascular Triad risk flag
    cv_triad = if_else(
      hypertension == "Yes" & heart_disease == "Yes" & age >= 60,
      "High_Risk", 
      "Standard"
    ) %>% factor(levels = c("Standard", "High_Risk"))
  )

# Verify clinical relevance of the cardiovascular triad
```

## Part 4: Imbalanced Learning & Tidymodels Pipeline (SMOTE)

### The Accuracy Paradox

In this cohort, $4,860$ out of $5,109$ patients ($95.13\%$) are stroke-free. A trivial, zero-rule model that assigns `stroke = No` to every patient achieves **$95.13\%$ accuracy** while possessing a **Sensitivity of $0\%$** [@he2009learning; @kuhn2013applied].

Because the clinical cost of a False Negative vastly exceeds that of a False Positive ($C_{FN} \gg C_{FP}$), models must be formulated to optimize **Sensitivity** and **AUC-ROC** rather than raw accuracy [@fawcett2006introduction].

### Synthetic Minority Over-sampling (SMOTE)

We employ **SMOTE** via the `themis` package [@chawla2002smote]. Rather than duplicating minority records, SMOTE synthesizes novel minority cases by identifying the $K$ nearest neighbors for each minority instance $\mathbf{x}_i \in S_{\text{min}}$ and interpolating in feature space:

$$\mathbf{x}_{\text{synthetic}} = \mathbf{x}_i + \lambda (\mathbf{x}_{zi} - \mathbf{x}_i), \quad \lambda \sim U(0, 1)$$

### Stratified Partitioning & Recipe Construction

```{r model-test-eval}
# Generate predicted class probabilities and class labels on test set
eval_model <- function(fitted_wf, model_label, test_df, thresh = 0.50) {
  probs <- predict(fitted_wf, new_data = test_df, type = "prob")
  
  df_res <- test_df %>%
    select(stroke) %>%
    bind_cols(probs) %>%
    mutate(
      .pred_class = factor(if_else(.pred_Yes >= thresh, "Yes", "No"), levels = c("No", "Yes")),
      Model = model_label,
      Threshold = thresh
    )
  
  # Calculate metrics using yardstick with event_level = "second" for "Yes"
  sens <- sens(df_res, truth = stroke, estimate = .pred_class, event_level = "second")$.estimate
  spec <- spec(df_res, truth = stroke, estimate = .pred_class, event_level = "second")$.estimate
  bacc <- bal_accuracy(df_res, truth = stroke, estimate = .pred_class, event_level = "second")$.estimate
  f1   <- f_meas(df_res, truth = stroke, estimate = .pred_class, event_level = "second")$.estimate
  auc  <- roc_auc(df_res, truth = stroke, .pred_Yes, event_level = "second")$.estimate
  
  list(
    results_df = df_res,
    summary = tibble(
      Model = model_label,
      Threshold = thresh,
      Sensitivity = round(sens, 3),
      Specificity = round(spec, 3),
      Balanced_Acc = round(bacc, 3),
      F1_Score = round(f1, 3),
      AUC_ROC = round(auc, 3)
    )
  )
}
```

## Part 6: Clinical Decision Threshold Calibration (Youden's J)

In clinical risk screening, setting the decision threshold to $p = 0.50$ is rarely optimal because event prevalence is low ($4.9\%$). Using the `pROC` package, we determine the optimal operating threshold via **Youden's $J$ statistic**, which maximizes the trade-off between sensitivity and specificity [@fawcett2006introduction]:

$$J = \max_p (\text{Sensitivity}(p) + \text{Specificity}(p) - 1)$$

```{r threshold-calibration}
# Calculate optimal threshold using pROC
proc_obj <- roc(res_m2$results_df$stroke, res_m2$results_df$.pred_Yes, levels = c("No", "Yes"), direction = "<")
best_coords <- coords(proc_obj, "best", best.method = "youden", ret = c("threshold", "sensitivity", "specificity"))

kable(best_coords, digits = 3, caption = "Table 4: Optimal Decision Threshold via Youden's J Index") %>%
  kable_styling(bootstrap_options = c("striped", "hover"), full_width = FALSE)
```

By calibrating the decision threshold to **$p^* \approx `r sprintf("%.2f", best_coords$threshold[1])`$** on the SMOTE Logistic Regression model, the pipeline achieves **$`r sprintf("%.1f", 100 * best_coords$sensitivity[1])`\%$ Sensitivity** and **$`r sprintf("%.1f", 100 * best_coords$specificity[1])`\%$ Specificity**, capturing roughly `r round(10 * best_coords$sensitivity[1])` in every 10 stroke patients in the held-out test cohort.

```{ojs}
function derive(x) {
  return {
    ...x,

                : x.avg_glucose_level < 126 ? "Prediabetes" : "Diabetes",
    cv_triad: x.hypertension === "Yes" && x.heart_disease === "Yes" && x.age >= 60

function score(x) {
  const parts = terms.map(t => {
    const raw = t.kind === "numeric" ? x[t.variable] : (x[t.variable] === t.level ? 1 : 0);
    return {term: t.term, contribution: t.coef * (raw - t.mean) / t.sd};
  });

  return {eta, p: 1 / (1 + Math.exp(-eta)), parts};
```

Why Observable JS rather than Shinylive? A logistic regression is a dot product plus a sigmoid, so the browser can reproduce the fitted model to floating-point precision (checked above) with a few kilobytes of exported numbers. Shinylive would download a WebAssembly R runtime and the `tidymodels` stack (tens of megabytes) to compute the same number; it becomes the right tool only for models that cannot be reduced to exported parameters, such as the random forest or XGBoost fits.

## Part 8: Epidemiological Findings & Methodological Takeaways

### Key Clinical Findings

1. **Age as the Primary Non-Modifiable Determinant:** Consistent with the Global Burden of Disease Stroke Study [@feigin2021global], patient age is the single most powerful predictor of cerebrovascular events. Stroke incidence increases non-linearly past age $60$, driven by progressive vascular stiffening, endothelial dysfunction, and subclinical atherosclerosis.
2. **Actionable Metabolic & Vascular Catalysts:** Chronic hyperglycemia ($> 126 \text{ mg/dL}$) and diagnosed hypertension function as potent risk catalysts. Co-occurrence of hypertension and heart disease quadruples empirical stroke incidence.
3. **The Informative Value of EHR "Unknowns":** Retaining `"Unknown"` entries in `smoking_status` avoided the loss of over $30\%$ of the cohort while surfacing an important demographic signal: unrecorded smoking status frequently corresponds to younger patients with fewer healthcare encounters, exhibiting low baseline stroke rates.

### Methodological Lessons in Medical Machine Learning

- **Abandoning Raw Classification Accuracy:** In rare-event clinical epidemiology ($4.9\%$ prevalence), standard accuracy is fundamentally flawed. Relying on default $0.50$ thresholds in unweighted models yielded $95.2\%$ accuracy but missed $`r sprintf("%.0f", 100 * (1 - res_m1$summary$Sensitivity))`\%$ of actual stroke events.
- **The Value of Synthetic Resampling (SMOTE):** Incorporating SMOTE within a unified `tidymodels` pipeline elevated model sensitivity from $`r sprintf("%.1f", 100 * res_m1$summary$Sensitivity)`\%$ to **$`r sprintf("%.1f", 100 * res_m2$summary$Sensitivity)`\%$** (both at $p = 0.50$), enabling the classifier to capture subtle physiological signals across the minority class.
- **Explainability vs. Ensembles:** Random Forest and XGBoost reached $\text{AUC} = `r sprintf("%.2f", res_m3$summary$AUC_ROC)`$ and $`r sprintf("%.2f", res_m4$summary$AUC_ROC)`$; **SMOTE-balanced Logistic Regression** discriminated better ($\text{AUC} = `r sprintf("%.2f", res_m2$summary$AUC_ROC)`$) while providing interpretable odds ratios that clinicians can review and understand at the bedside.
