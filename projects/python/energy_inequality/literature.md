## Literature review

### Domestic energy as a spatial question of justice

Research on household energy deprivation in the United Kingdom has long had a geographical side. Boardman's work on fuel poverty defined the problem as the meeting point of low income, high energy prices and an inefficient housing stock [@boardman2010fixing]. Each of these three conditions is unevenly distributed across space. Later scholarship recast fuel poverty as a matter of justice: who bears the burdens of the energy system, whose needs are recognised, and who has a voice in the decisions [@walker2012fuel; @jenkins2016energy]. @bouzarovski2017spatializing argue that injustice is produced in and through space. Housing stock, infrastructure networks and local labour markets combine to create "landscapes" of vulnerability that national averages hide. This spatial turn has practical consequences. Area-based targeting of support depends on knowing where need is concentrated. Yet the choice of indicator alters both the map and the population identified as fuel poor [@robinson2018getting]. Small-area prediction models have been used to estimate fuel poverty below the local-authority scale [@fahmy2011predicting]. Geographically weighted principal components analysis shows that the drivers of vulnerability themselves vary between neighbourhoods [@robinson2019spatially]. Rural households off the gas grid, often heating with electricity or oil, face a distinct risk profile from urban households [@roberts2015fuel].

Distributional analysis of energy use borrows its tools from the study of income. @jacobson2005letting applied Lorenz curves and Gini coefficients to cumulative electricity consumption, arguing that these instruments measure equity in energy services. The analogy needs care at the small-area scale. Electricity consumption is not a welfare measure: high use per meter can reflect affluence and appliance ownership, but also electric resistance heating in poorly insulated homes. Low use can signal efficiency, rooftop solar generation or under-consumption by households rationing energy [@fawcett2024using]. Any account of spatial inequality in metered electricity must therefore separate the level of consumption from its composition and drivers.

### Drivers of household electricity demand

Dwelling-level studies agree on a broad hierarchy of determinants. For English homes heated by gas, @huebner2016understanding found that appliance ownership and use explained the largest share of variation in annual electricity consumption. Socio-demographic factors explained around a fifth, led by household size, while building characteristics mattered little once heating was excluded. When total energy is modelled, building factors such as floor area and dwelling type dominate [@huebner2015explaining]. Analyses of English dwelling stock confirm the joint importance of physical and socio-economic characteristics, including tenure and income [@wyatt2013dwelling]. Geographically and socio-economically disaggregated models show large differences between neighbourhood types [@druckman2008household]. Weather is a further first-order driver, especially where electricity provides heating, and benchmarking models routinely include degree days [@summerfield2010two].

Energy Performance Certificates (EPCs) are the most widely used proxy for dwelling efficiency in England and Wales, and the register is openly available [@mhclg2025epc]. Their use as an explanatory variable is contested. The register has recording errors and inconsistencies [@hardy2019analysis]. Modelled demand diverges systematically from measured demand, and the gap widens as ratings worsen [@summerfield2019empirical]. Using smart-meter data, @few2023overprediction show that EPCs over-predict primary energy use by about 8% in band C and nearly 50% in bands F and G, even in homes whose occupancy matches the model's assumptions. This echoes the "prebound effect", in which occupants of inefficient homes consume much less than models predict [@sunikkablank2012introducing]. EPC ratings therefore measure modelled performance, not realised consumption. Their area-level aggregates also inherit the selective coverage of the register, which records only dwellings sold, let or newly built since 2008.

Few studies examine how these relationships vary across space. @feng2025examining apply multiscale geographically weighted regression to LSOAs in Nottingham and find that drivers operate at different spatial scales, with residual spatial autocorrelation remaining after modelling. @netobradley2023microsimulation use a Bayesian multilevel microsimulation to show how household practice, local socio-cultural context and ward-level spatial effects jointly shape fuel use in South Indian cities. Their argument that spatial context conditions energy transitions travels well beyond that setting. Nationally comprehensive evidence of this kind for England and Wales, followed over time, is largely missing.

### Measuring consumption at small-area scale

The Department for Energy Security and Net Zero (DESNZ) publishes annual meter-level electricity consumption aggregated to Lower Layer Super Output Areas (LSOAs) [@desnz2025lsoa]. The methodology booklet sets out several properties that shape analysis [@desnz2026subnational]. Domestic consumption covers non-half-hourly meters on profile classes 1 and 2 (standard and Economy 7 tariffs), with an automatic reallocation above 100,000 kWh. The reference period runs from late January to late January rather than the calendar year. Electricity, unlike gas, is not weather-corrected. About a fifth of readings are estimated rather than actual. Meters do not map one-to-one onto households. Statistics from 2015 onwards are revised each year with consistent postcode information and published on Census 2021 geographies. The earlier years were compiled on 2011 boundaries. Researchers using a 2010-onwards panel therefore need to harmonise the early years and should test for a break around 2015.

LSOAs were designed for statistical stability and comparability, with automated zone design used to keep the system consistent across censuses [@cockings2011maintaining]. After the 2021 Census, around 1,000 of the 2011 LSOAs in England and Wales were split, merged or irregularly redrawn. The Office for National Statistics publishes an exact-fit lookup that classifies each change [@ons2021statgeog; @ons2023lsoalookup]. Any zonal system is open to the modifiable areal unit problem. Statistical relationships, including correlation coefficients and regression parameters, can change with the scale and arrangement of the zones [@openshaw1984modifiable; @fotheringham1991modifiable]. At the LSOA scale this affects how much inequality is "within" versus "between" local authorities. It also means that associations with Census covariates are ecological: they describe places, not households.

### Spatial inequality and exploratory spatial data analysis

Exploratory spatial data analysis (ESDA) gives a first account of whether consumption is spatially structured. Moran's *I* [@moran1950notes] summarises global spatial autocorrelation. Local indicators of spatial association (LISA) break it down into location-specific clusters and outliers [@anselin1995local]. Because local tests are many and dependent, significance needs adjustment, for example by controlling the false discovery rate [@caldasdecastro2006controlling]. Choropleth classification affects what a map appears to show. The Fisher–Jenks optimal classification [@fisher1958grouping; @jenks1967data] minimises within-class variance. With very large numbers of observations, sampling strategies are needed to keep it tractable [@rey2017evaluation]. Kernel density estimation [@silverman1986density] gives a classification-free view of the distribution's shape, which matters when looking for multi-modality.

Inequality across places is measured with indices borrowed from income analysis [@cowell2011measuring]. The Theil index [@theil1967economics] belongs to the class of additively decomposable measures [@shorrocks1980class], so total inequality splits exactly into between-group and within-group components. Here the groups are local authority districts or regions. The Gini coefficient is not additively decomposable in this sense, but @rey2013spatial show how it can be split into contributions from neighbouring and non-neighbouring pairs, giving an explicitly spatial reading of inequality. @rey2025spatial reviews these a-spatial and spatial measures and stresses reproducible computation. The PySAL ecosystem implements most of them in open-source Python [@rey2022pysal].

### Distribution dynamics, convergence and place mobility

Static snapshots cannot show whether places change position within the distribution. The distribution-dynamics approach of Quah [-@quah1996twin; -@quah1997empirics] models the evolution of the entire cross-sectional distribution with Markov transition matrices and stochastic kernels. This approach can reveal stratification or "twin peaks" that regression-based convergence tests miss. @rey2001spatial extended it with the spatial Markov chain, which conditions transition probabilities on the class of a unit's spatial lag. Neighbourhood context can then be tested as a factor in upward or downward mobility. Formal tests of the spatial-Markov property and its power have since been developed [@rey2016properties; @kang2018conditional]. The Markov assumption itself needs checking, including homogeneity over time and space [@bickenbach2003evaluating]. LISA Markov chains and directional rose diagrams show how units move jointly with their neighbours through the Moran scatterplot [@rey2011visualizing]. Local indicators of mobility association identify where rank changes cluster [@rey2016space]. @rey2023inequality give an accessible computational account of these tools.

Parametric convergence analysis offers a complementary view. The log *t* test of @phillips2007transition and the clustering algorithm of @phillips2009economic identify "convergence clubs": groups of units that converge to different steady states, without assuming common factors are homogeneous. The method has been applied to European regions [@bartkowska2012regional] and is available in standard software [@sichera2019convergenceclubs]. Within energy research, @herrerias2017residential examined convergence in residential energy consumption across Chinese regions. @tastan2023club identified convergence clubs in city-level electricity consumption in Turkey. Neither study works at the neighbourhood scale, and neither addresses spatial dependence between clubs.

Place mobility links inequality to dynamics. Building on rank-mobility measurement [@shorrocks1978measurement], @vankerm2004what decomposed mobility into a *growth* component (a general shift in levels), a *dispersion* component (change in the spread of the distribution) and an *exchange* component (re-ranking). @rey2010interregional and @rey2023place apply this reasoning to Mexican regions and municipios. They show that rising spatial dependence and changes in inequality can coexist with substantial exchange mobility, and that the growth component can reflect national shocks. To our knowledge, this decomposition has not been applied to energy consumption.

### Explaining spatial heterogeneity

Where ESDA and Markov analysis describe pattern and change, explanatory models ask whether the drivers of consumption vary across space and time. Geographically weighted regression [@brunsdon1996geographically] estimates locally varying coefficients. Its multiscale generalisation (MGWR) gives each covariate its own bandwidth, so processes operating at different scales can be separated [@fotheringham2017multiscale; @oshan2019mgwr]. Spatio-temporal extensions add a temporal kernel [@huang2010geographically; @fotheringham2015geographical]. Good practice for these models covers bandwidth selection, inference on local coefficients and collinearity [@comber2023route]. Fixed Rank Kriging provides a scalable alternative for very large spatial and spatio-temporal datasets. It represents the trend surface with a reduced set of multi-resolution basis functions [@cressie2008fixed] and is implemented for Gaussian and non-Gaussian data [@zammitmangion2021frk; @sainsburydale2024modeling].

### Shocks and transitions: pandemic, price crisis and electrification

The study period contains three disruptions that any dynamic analysis must address. First, COVID-19 restrictions moved activity into homes. Across Europe, lockdowns shifted electricity demand from commercial to residential uses [@bahmanyar2020impact]. In England and Wales, a counterfactual analysis of smart-meter data estimated that household electricity use rose by 7.8% in the first pandemic year and 2.2% in the second, with the largest increases among households with children [@zapatawebborn2023impact]. Second, the 2022 energy-price crisis raised the household burden worldwide [@guan2023burden]. In Great Britain, winter 2022/23 electricity consumption was 8.4% below the previous winter after weather adjustment. The largest reductions were associated with underheating and fuel poverty [@zapatawebborn2024winter]. Analyses of prepayment customers likewise show limited further capacity to cut demand among the poorest households [@fawcett2024using]. The SERL Observatory, which links smart-meter, survey and EPC data, underpins much of this evidence [@webborn2021serl]. Third, electrification is changing what metered electricity means. Heat pumps add substantial and highly seasonal load [@love2017addition], and their uptake shows strong local clustering and spillovers [@arvanitopoulos2025decarbonising]. Rooftop solar reduces metered imports through self-consumption [@mckenna2018solar], and its deployment is itself spatially patterned [@baltaozkan2015regional]. Over the 2010s and 2020s, convergence or divergence in per-meter consumption may therefore reflect the uneven arrival of low-carbon technologies as well as changes in need or affordability.

### Research gap

Four gaps follow from this literature. First, UK evidence on the drivers of domestic electricity demand comes mostly from household samples or single cities. No study has described the full LSOA-level distribution for England and Wales over more than a decade with spatially explicit methods. Second, distribution-dynamics, club-convergence and place-mobility methods are well developed for regional income but almost unused for neighbourhood energy consumption. As a result, we do not know whether places keep their relative positions, whether neighbourhood context affects their movement, or whether the distribution is splitting into distinct clubs. Third, the spatial-justice literature emphasises local variation in vulnerability, but the spatial non-stationarity of consumption drivers has been examined only locally. Fourth, the pandemic, the price crisis and electrification are well documented in smart-meter panels, but their small-area spatial footprint, and whether they compressed or widened gaps between places, remain unknown.

### Research questions and hypotheses

**RQ1 (spatial structure).** Is per-meter domestic electricity consumption in England and Wales spatially clustered at the LSOA scale, and how much of its inequality lies within rather than between local authorities?
*H1:* Global Moran's *I* is positive and significant in every year. The within-LAD component dominates a Theil decomposition. Within-LAD dispersion is systematically related to LAD mean consumption.

**RQ2 (distribution dynamics and place mobility).** Do LSOAs keep their relative positions over time, and is upward or downward movement conditioned by neighbourhood context?
*H2:* Classic Markov transition matrices show high persistence on the diagonal. Spatial Markov tests reject spatial homogeneity, with low-consumption neighbourhoods reducing upward mobility. In a place-mobility decomposition, the growth component dominates in shock years (2020, 2022) and exchange mobility outweighs dispersion.

**RQ3 (convergence clubs).** Is consumption converging nationally, or are LSOAs forming distinct convergence clubs, and are those clubs spatially concentrated?
*H3:* The Phillips–Sul log *t* test rejects global convergence. Club membership is spatially autocorrelated and associated with heating fuel (off-gas-grid and Economy 7 areas) and dwelling type.

**RQ4 (heterogeneous drivers).** Do dwelling, tenure, household and efficiency characteristics relate to consumption uniformly across space, or do their associations vary at different spatial scales?
*H4:* In MGWR, household size and the share of detached dwellings operate at near-global bandwidths. Heating-fuel and EPC-efficiency effects are local, and remain so after accounting for spatial autocorrelation.

**RQ5 (shocks and transitions).** Did the COVID-19 period and the 2022 price crisis compress or widen spatial inequality in domestic electricity consumption?
*H5:* Relative inequality fell in 2022 as high-consuming, electrically heated areas cut demand most. Low-consumption, more deprived areas show the smallest reductions, consistent with limited room for further rationing.
